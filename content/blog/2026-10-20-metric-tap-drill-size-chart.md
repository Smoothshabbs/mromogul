Most tapping problems, such as broken taps, torn threads and loose bolts, start with the wrong drill size. Here is the chart for metric coarse threads, with a few habits that make tapping easier.

## Tap drill sizes: metric coarse (ISO)

The standard tap drill size is the **thread size minus the pitch**. For example, M10 × 1.5 needs an 8.5 mm drill.

| Thread | Pitch (mm) | Tap drill (mm) |
|---|---|---|
| M3 | 0.50 | 2.5 |
| M4 | 0.70 | 3.3 |
| M5 | 0.80 | 4.2 |
| M6 | 1.00 | 5.0 |
| M8 | 1.25 | 6.8 |
| M10 | 1.50 | 8.5 |
| M12 | 1.75 | 10.2 |
| M14 | 2.00 | 12.0 |
| M16 | 2.00 | 14.0 |
| M18 | 2.50 | 15.5 |
| M20 | 2.50 | 17.5 |
| M22 | 2.50 | 19.5 |
| M24 | 3.00 | 21.0 |

In hard or tough materials, some machinists go slightly larger (for example 8.6 mm for M10) to reduce the load on the tap. The thread is a little shallower but still strong enough for most uses.

## Taper, second and bottoming taps

Hand taps come in sets of three:

- **Taper (first):** a long lead that starts the thread easily and straight. For through holes, this one is often enough.
- **Second (intermediate):** continues the thread.
- **Bottoming (plug):** almost no lead, to cut full threads right to the bottom of a blind hole.

For production work on a drill or tapping machine, a **spiral point tap** pushes chips ahead of the tap. That makes it ideal for through holes and much less likely to jam.

## Tips for clean threads and unbroken taps

1. **Drill straight,** and chamfer the hole slightly so the tap starts centred.
2. **Keep the tap square** to the work. A tap guide or the drill press helps.
3. **Use cutting oil** on steel. Cast iron and brass can be tapped dry.
4. **By hand, turn forward half a turn, then back a quarter turn** to break the chip.
5. **In a blind hole, stop early and clear the chips** before using the bottoming tap.
6. **Stop and reverse if it gets tight.** Forcing the tap is how it breaks.
